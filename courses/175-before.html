Understanding Michigan's Implicit Bias Training Requirement

Michigan's implicit bias training requirement was established to address healthcare disparities and ensure all patients receive equitable care regardless of race, ethnicity, gender, age, socioeconomic status, or other characteristics. The mandate applies to all health professionals licensed under the Michigan Public Health Code (Act 368 of 1978), Article 15.

What Michigan Administrative Rule R 338.7004 Requires

Effective June 1, 2022, Michigan Administrative Rule R 338.7004 establishes specific requirements for implicit bias training:

R 338.7004(1): Initial Licensure Requirement

An individual seeking initial licensure or registration must complete a minimum of 2 hours of implicit bias training within the 5 years immediately preceding issuance of the license or registration.

R 338.7004(2): Renewal Requirement

For license renewal, each licensee must complete a minimum of 1 hour of implicit bias training per calendar year. A 2-hour course satisfies your requirement for 2 renewal years and counts toward your total CE hours.

R 338.7004(3): Required Training Content

Training must include information on implicit bias, diversity, equity, and inclusion. Specifically, the training must address: the definition and recognition of implicit bias, how implicit bias affects perceptions and treatment decisions, strategies for reducing the negative effects of implicit bias, and approaches to promote equity in healthcare delivery.

R 338.7004(4): Approved Provider Standards

Training may be obtained from: (a) a nationally-recognized or state-recognized health-related organization, (b) an educational program approved by any Michigan licensing board, or (c) a program sponsored by a hospital, medical society, or professional association. Renew Now CE qualifies under (a) and (b) as an ANCC-accredited provider approved by the Michigan Boards of Nursing and Pharmacy.

What Is Implicit Bias in Healthcare?

Implicit bias refers to unconscious attitudes, stereotypes, or assumptions that affect our understanding, actions, and decisions without our conscious awareness. Unlike explicit bias (which is deliberate), implicit bias operates automatically and can influence clinical decision-making even among well-intentioned healthcare providers.

Research has documented that implicit bias can affect:

  • Pain assessment and management — Studies show disparities in pain medication prescribing based on patient race
  • Diagnostic accuracy — Unconscious assumptions can lead to delayed or missed diagnoses
  • Treatment recommendations — Patients may receive different care recommendations based on demographic characteristics
  • Patient-provider communication — Implicit bias can affect rapport, trust, and treatment adherence
  • Referral patterns — Disparities in specialist referrals have been linked to implicit bias
